Optimization of a Rectangular Microstrip Patch Antenna
Using GA and Surrogate Assisted PSO

EE5606 Artificial Intelligence for Antennas in Wireless Communication

1 Introduction and design objective

This project minimizes the effective volume of a rectangular microstrip patch antenna operating near 2.4 GHz. Part I uses a genetic algorithm (GA) with the analytical transmission-line model. Part II trains a neural network (NN) and a Gaussian process (GP), then uses each fixed predictor in particle swarm optimization (PSO). Final designs are checked with the analytical model. The recorded experiments produce volumes near 1057 mm^3; the GP gives lower frequency prediction error than the NN. Full-wave simulation is outside the worksheet requirements [1].

The design vector is x = [W, L, h], where W is patch width, L is patch length and h is substrate thickness. The bounds are 25 ≤ W ≤ 50 mm, 25 ≤ L ≤ 45 mm and 1 ≤ h ≤ 5 mm, with W/L ≥ 1.2. The substrate relative permittivity is ε_r = 4.4 and c = 3 × 10^8 m/s. Volume is V = WLh in mm^3.

2 Analytical model and common objective

The worksheet model accounts for the effective dielectric constant and the fringing extension at both ends of the patch [1]:

ε_eff = (ε_r + 1)/2 + [(ε_r − 1)/2](1 + 12h/W)^−1/2

ΔL = 0.412h [(ε_eff + 0.3)(W/h + 0.264)]
/ [(ε_eff − 0.258)(W/h + 0.8)]

L_eff = L + 2ΔL,     f = c / (2L_eff√ε_eff)

Dimensions use consistent units in the ratios; the effective length is converted to metres before calculating frequency in hertz, then converted to GHz. The reference design [38, 30, 1.6] mm gives 2.357548542 GHz and 1824 mm^3.

The compared experiments minimize the same penalized objective:

J(x) = V(x)/11250 + λ |f_eval(x) − 2.4|,     λ = 1

Here f_eval is numerically expressed in GHz and is supplied by the analytical model for GA or the trained surrogate for PSO. The volume scale 11250 mm^3 is the product of the rectangular upper bounds. The penalty weight and acceptance tolerance |f_analytical − 2.4| ≤ 0.01 GHz (10 MHz) are experimental choices, not worksheet requirements. Success requires both this tolerance and valid geometry.

3 Methodology

Analytical GA. Each chromosome stores [W, L, h]. Tournament selection favors lower objective values, arithmetic crossover blends parent dimensions, and Gaussian mutation perturbs individual coordinates. Elitism retains the best candidate. Dimensions are clipped and repaired to satisfy the bounds and width-to-length ratio. The selected recorded GA study uses population 6, tournament size 2, crossover probability 0.8, per-coordinate mutation probability 0.2, mutation scale 0.1 of each coordinate range, and one elite. It runs at least 100 generations, then stops when the relative best-score change is below 0.01. The ten recorded seeds are 42 and 0 to 8; each run completed 100 generations [2].

Data and scaling. Uniform random samples are drawn from the rectangular bounds, rejecting W/L < 1.2. The analytical formula labels 600 accepted designs. Sampling and split seeds are both 1. A shared 70/15/15 split provides 420 training, 90 validation and 90 test samples. Inputs and frequency labels are standardized using training-set means and standard deviations only. Validation data guide model selection; the test set measures prediction accuracy. Saved predictors accept millimetres and return GHz after reversing the output scaling [3].

Neural network. The selected model has three inputs, one hidden layer of 32 tanh neurons and one linear output, giving 161 trainable parameters. In standardized coordinates its mapping is:

ŷ = W_2 tanh(W_1x̃ + b_1) + b_2

Mean squared error is minimized by manually implemented backpropagation and full-batch gradient descent. A strong Wolfe line search selects the step length, starting from 1.0 with sufficient-decrease parameter 10^−4, curvature parameter 0.1 and a 40-iteration search limit. The saved run uses initialization seed 1 and 30,000 epochs; the best validation checkpoint is epoch 29,998. It stops at the epoch limit, without reaching its 10 kHz validation RMSE goal. CuPy supplies GPU array operations for the recorded NN timing [3, 5].

Gaussian process. The custom NumPy implementation uses an RBF kernel on standardized inputs:

k(x̃, z̃) = σ_f^2 exp[−||x̃ − z̃||^2/(2ℓ^2)]

The training covariance is C = K + (σ_n^2 + j)I. Cholesky factorization and linear solves obtain α from Cα = ỹ; predictions use k_*^Tα and are converted back to GHz. Validation RMSE selects ℓ from {0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8} and σ_n from {10^−6, 10^−4}, with σ_f = 1. The selected values are ℓ = 4 and σ_n = 10^−6, with jitter j = 10^−10. These small diagonal terms stabilize deterministic analytical data; they are not measured antenna noise [4].

3 Methodology continued

Surrogate PSO. Each particle stores [W, L, h]. Personal and global best positions guide velocity, using independent random vectors r_1 and r_2 with entries uniform on [0, 1]. Products below act coordinate by coordinate:

v_t+1 = 0.7v_t + 1.6r_1 × (p_best − x_t)
+ 1.6r_2 × (g_best − x_t)

x_t+1 = repair(x_t + v_t+1)

Both surrogate experiments use 40 particles, seeds 1 to 5, and velocity limits of 20% of each coordinate range. Recorded stopping settings are a minimum of 150 and maximum of 1000 iterations, with a relative best-score change threshold of 0.01. All runs complete 150 iterations and 6040 objective evaluations. The trained predictor remains fixed during search. The final analytical frequency is used to assess acceptance separately from the surrogate objective [5].

5 Discussion

GP test RMSE is approximately 83 times lower than NN test RMSE. The RBF model closely represents this smooth, deterministic function, while the NN supports designs within the 10 MHz tolerance. GP reduces seed-1 volume by only 0.063% relative to NN; its main advantage is accuracy. Both PSO methods approach the minimum thickness and width-to-length-ratio boundaries. This is consistent with minimizing volume, but establishes neither global optimality nor radiation efficiency.

GP fitting and hyperparameter selection took 2.583 s. Prediction time averaged over 100 warm calls was 0.482 ms for the GPU NN and 0.0512 ms for the CPU GP. Different backends limit this comparison. Dataset generation and NN training times were not recorded. Missing costs, unequal search budgets and the inexpensive analytical formula prevent a justified overall speedup claim. PSO seeds vary search randomness only, not surrogate training.

6 Conclusion

Analytical GA and both surrogate PSO experiments yield valid designs near 2.4 GHz and approximately 1057 mm^3. GP is the more accurate surrogate; both surrogate methods meet the chosen tolerance after analytical verification. These results demonstrate the worksheet workflow. Gain, bandwidth, impedance matching and full-wave performance remain outside this evaluation.
