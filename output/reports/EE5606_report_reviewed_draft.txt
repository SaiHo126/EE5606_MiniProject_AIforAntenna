Optimization of a Rectangular Microstrip Patch Antenna
Using GA and Surrogate Assisted PSO

EE5606 Artificial Intelligence for Antennas in Wireless Communication

1 Introduction and design objective

This project minimizes the effective volume of a rectangular microstrip patch antenna operating near 2.4 GHz. Part I uses a genetic algorithm (GA) with the analytical transmission-line model. Part II trains a neural network (NN) and a Gaussian process (GP), then uses each fixed predictor in particle swarm optimization (PSO). Analytical verification of the saved experiments gives volumes near 1057 mm^3. Both surrogates meet the chosen frequency tolerance, while GP gives lower prediction error. Full-wave simulation is outside the worksheet requirements [1].

The design vector is x = [W, L, h], where the entries are patch width, patch length and substrate thickness. The bounds are 25 ≤ W ≤ 50 mm, 25 ≤ L ≤ 45 mm and 1 ≤ h ≤ 5 mm, with W/L ≥ 1.2. The substrate has relative permittivity ε_r = 4.4, and c = 3 × 10^8 m/s. Effective volume is V = WLh in mm^3.

2 Analytical model and common objective

The worksheet model accounts for effective permittivity and the fringing extension at both ends of the patch [1]:

ε_eff = (ε_r + 1)/2 + [(ε_r − 1)/2](1 + 12h/W)^−1/2

ΔL = 0.412h [(ε_eff + 0.3)(W/h + 0.264)]
/ [(ε_eff − 0.258)(W/h + 0.8)]

L_eff = L + 2ΔL,    f = c / (2L_eff√ε_eff)

Lengths are consistent within the ratios; effective length is converted to metres before calculating frequency in hertz, then converted to GHz. The reference design [38, 30, 1.6] mm gives 2.357548542 GHz and 1824 mm^3.

J(x) = V(x)/(11250 mm^3) + λ |f_eval(x) − 2.4 GHz|

All compared runs use λ = 1 GHz^−1. The volume scale is the product of the rectangular upper bounds. GA uses analytical frequency; PSO uses surrogate frequency. The acceptance tolerance |f_analytical − 2.4 GHz| ≤ 0.01 GHz (10 MHz) and penalty weight are experimental choices, not worksheet requirements. Success requires both this tolerance and valid geometry.

3 Methodology

Analytical GA. Each chromosome stores [W, L, h]. Tournament selection favors lower scores; arithmetic crossover blends parents; Gaussian mutation perturbs individual coordinates. Elitism retains the best candidate. Clipping and geometry repair enforce the bounds and width-to-length ratio. The selected archive uses population 6, tournament size 2, crossover probability 0.8, mutation probability 0.2 per coordinate, mutation scale 0.1 of each coordinate range, and one elite. After at least 100 generations, a relative best-score change below 0.01 ends the search. The ten actual seeds are 42 and 0 to 8; each run completed 100 generations [2].

Data and scaling. Uniform samples are drawn from the rectangular bounds, rejecting W/L < 1.2. The analytical model labels 600 accepted designs. Sampling and split seeds are both 1. A common 70/15/15 split gives 420 training, 90 validation and 90 test samples. Input dimensions and frequency labels are standardized using training-set means and standard deviations only. Validation data guide model selection; the held-out test set measures prediction accuracy. Saved predictors accept millimetres and return GHz after reversing the output scaling [3, 4].

Neural network. The selected model has three inputs, one hidden layer of 32 tanh neurons and a linear output, giving 161 trainable parameters. In standardized coordinates:

ŷ = W_2 tanh(W_1x̃ + b_1) + b_2

Mean squared error is minimized with manually implemented backpropagation and full-batch gradient descent. A strong Wolfe line search selects the step length, with initial step 1.0, sufficient-decrease parameter 10^−4, curvature parameter 0.1 and 40 iterations per search phase. Initialization uses seed 1. The saved run reaches 30,000 epochs, with its best validation checkpoint at epoch 29,998. It stops at the epoch limit without reaching its 10 kHz validation RMSE goal [3].

Gaussian process. The custom NumPy implementation uses a radial basis function (RBF) kernel on standardized inputs:

k(x̃, z̃) = σ_f^2 exp[−||x̃ − z̃||^2/(2ℓ^2)]

C = K + (σ_n^2 + j)I,    Cα = ỹ,    ŷ_* = k_*^Tα

Cholesky factorization and linear solves obtain α without explicitly inverting the covariance matrix. The prediction is converted back to GHz. Validation RMSE selects the length scale from {0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8} and noise standard deviation from {0.000001, 0.0001}, with signal standard deviation 1. The selected length scale is 4, noise standard deviation 0.000001, and jitter 0.0000000001. These diagonal terms stabilize deterministic analytical data; they are not measured antenna noise [4].

3 Methodology continued

Surrogate PSO. Each particle stores [W, L, h]. Personal and global best positions guide velocity. Independent random vectors r_1 and r_2 have entries uniform on [0, 1]. Products below act coordinate by coordinate:

v_t+1 = 0.7v_t + 1.6r_1 × (p_best − x_t)
+ 1.6r_2 × (g_best − x_t)

x_t+1 = repair(x_t + v_t+1)

Both archived comparisons use 40 particles, seeds 1 to 5, and velocity limits of 20% of each dimension range. The recorded stopping settings are 150 minimum and 1000 maximum iterations, with a relative best-score change threshold of 0.01. Every run completes 150 iterations and 6040 objective evaluations. The trained predictor remains fixed during search; final analytical verification is separate from the surrogate score [5].

5 Discussion

GP test RMSE is about 83 times lower than NN test RMSE, but its seed-1 volume is only 0.063% smaller. Its main advantage here is accuracy. Designs approach the minimum thickness and ratio boundaries. Global optimality is unproven, including minimum volume throughout the accepted frequency band: the objective also penalizes departure from exactly 2.4 GHz.

GP fitting and selection took 2.583 s. Mean warm prediction times over 100 calls were 0.482 ms for the GPU NN (CuPy) and 0.0512 ms for the CPU GP (NumPy). Different backends limit comparison. Dataset generation, NN training and selected GA search times were not recorded; an overall speedup over the inexpensive analytical formula is therefore unproven. Success rates describe only the tested seeds, and PSO repeats use fixed trained models [4, 5].

Recalculation confirms saved prediction errors and analytical frequencies. The current PSO working file has incomplete initialization and evaluation code, however, and must be reconciled with the archived experiments before reproduction. These results describe saved runs.

6 Conclusion

Analytical GA and both surrogate PSO experiments yield valid designs near 2.4 GHz and 1057 mm^3. GP is more accurate; both surrogates meet the chosen tolerance after analytical verification. Gain, bandwidth, impedance matching and full-wave performance are outside this evaluation.
