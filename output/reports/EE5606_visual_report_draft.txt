Rectangular Microstrip Patch Antenna Optimization

EE5606 Artificial Intelligence for Antennas in Wireless Communication

1 Aim and analytical model

The aim is to minimize effective antenna volume while maintaining resonance near 2.4 GHz. Part I uses an analytical genetic algorithm (GA); Part II trains neural-network (NN) and Gaussian-process (GP) surrogates and uses particle swarm optimization (PSO). The implementation emphasis is the manually developed GeneticAlgorithm.py, NeuralNetwork.py and PSO.py. GP is a supporting comparison implemented with scikit-learn, rather than the main algorithm-development focus. The NN-GP comparison remains a worksheet requirement [1].

The design is [W, L, h] in mm, with W in [25, 50], L in [25, 45], h in [1, 5] and W/L ≥ 1.2. FR-4 is represented by ε_r = 4.4 and c = 3 × 10^8 m/s. The worksheet transmission-line equations are [1]:

ε_eff = (ε_r + 1)/2 + [(ε_r − 1)/2](1 + 12h/W)^−1/2

ΔL = 0.412h [(ε_eff + 0.3)(W/h + 0.264)]
/ [(ε_eff − 0.258)(W/h + 0.8)]

L_eff = L + 2ΔL,       f = c/(2L_eff√ε_eff),       V = WLh

The effective length is converted to metres for frequency calculation; the public interfaces return GHz and volume is in mm^3. The reference [38, 30, 1.6] mm gives 2.357548542 GHz and 1824 mm^3.

2 Shared objective and acceptance

J = V/11250 + λ |f_eval − 2.4|,       λ = 1

Here f_eval is the analytical or surrogate frequency in GHz. The scale 11250 mm^3, penalty λ and acceptance tolerance ±0.01 GHz are design choices. Geometry must be valid and analytical target deviation must be ≤10 MHz; a low surrogate score alone does not establish success.

3 Genetic algorithm and neural network implementation

GA. Each chromosome holds [W, L, h]. Tournament selection chooses lower-score parents, arithmetic crossover blends them using one random coefficient, and Gaussian mutation perturbs each coordinate. Geometry repair enforces the bounds and W/L constraint. One distinct elite is retained; duplicates are replaced with fresh feasible designs. The saved study uses population 6, tournament size 2, crossover probability 0.8, mutation probability 0.2 and mutation scale 10% of each coordinate range. It stops below 1% best-score change after at least 100 generations. Ten seeds (42 and 0-8) all satisfy analytical acceptance [2].

Figure 2. Saved GA histories; the darker curve is the lowest-score run (seed 5). All ten runs completed 100 generations.

Dataset. Uniform sampling rejects W/L < 1.2. The 600 feasible designs are labelled analytically and split into 420 training, 90 validation and 90 test samples, with sampling and split seeds both 1. Scaling uses training-set statistics only; both surrogates share the split.

Figure 3. Saved NN architecture. Scaling is retained with the weights, so prediction inputs remain in mm and outputs in GHz.

NN. The fully connected 3-32-1 model uses tanh hidden neurons, a linear output and Xavier initialization. For N samples, X has shape (N, 3), hidden activations (32, N), and output (N, 1). Its 161 weights and biases are trained with handwritten forward propagation, mean squared error, backpropagation and parameter updates:

z_y = W_2 tanh(W_1x̃ + b_1) + b_2,       θ ← θ − α grad(MSE)

Training uses standardized targets; prediction reverses target scaling to return GHz.

Backpropagation uses tanh derivative 1 − a^2 and sums each bias gradient over samples. A strong Wolfe line search chooses α with initial trial 1, decrease coefficient 10^−4 and curvature coefficient 0.1, using bracketing and bisection with a 40-iteration limit. Each update uses all 420 training samples. GPU array operations use CuPy; no automatic differentiation is used.

4 Surrogate training and prediction accuracy

The saved NN uses initialization seed 1 and completes 30,000 epochs; the best validation checkpoint is epoch 29,998. Training stops at the epoch limit, without reaching the 10 kHz validation goal. Other stopping conditions are 2000 epochs without improvement or a stalled line search. The best checkpoint is restored before testing [3].

Figure 4. NN training/validation RMSE and signed test error (prediction minus analytical frequency), from saved histories and predictions. Test size: 90.

GP benchmark. Following GPR.py, scikit-learn fits training-only StandardScaler and an RBF GaussianProcessRegressor: initial length scales [1, 1, 1], alpha = 10^−8, normalize_y = True. Marginal-likelihood optimization [5] gives scales [11.3, 2.26, 4.74]. Fitting took 0.183 s. Settings were fixed before testing; the NN checkpoint was reused without retraining [4].

GP has approximately 87 times lower test RMSE. The NN is nevertheless accurate enough for the selected final-design tolerance. Test error measures approximation quality, whereas final analytical target deviation measures whether optimization meets 2.4 GHz; these are different quantities.

5 Particle swarm optimization

PSO.py keeps the surrogate fixed and accepts an objective callback. Forty repaired particles store personal/global bests. Velocity is updated and clipped to 20% of each coordinate range; positions are moved, repaired and scored before updating the best records. Both models use seeds 1-5, inertia 0.7 and cognitive/social coefficients 1.6. Stopping uses <1% improvement after 150 iterations, capped at 1000. Every run completed 150 iterations and 6040 evaluations [4].

v ← 0.7v + 1.6r_1 × (p_best − x) + 1.6r_2 × (g_best − x)

Then x ← repair(x + v); products are elementwise and r_1, r_2 are uniform random vectors on [0, 1].

The table uses best-score GA and seed-1 PSO, with five-seed mean times from the new CPU comparison. Success rates are GA 10/10, NN 5/5 and GP 5/5. All designs have h = 1 mm and W/L = 1.2. Both surrogate predictions are ≈2.4 GHz, giving prediction errors of 0.767812 MHz (NN) and 0.057319 MHz (GP).

GPU latency. I initially loaded the trained NN on GPU for PSO. Five-seed searches averaged 2.080 s versus 0.142 s on CPU with identical weights: 14.6× longer [6]. Each particle prediction transfers three inputs to GPU; returning a Python float synchronizes the CPU-visible result. Repeated transfers, kernel launches and synchronization can dominate this tiny network. Transfer delay was not separately profiled, so this remains the likely explanation. CPU inference requires no retraining; batching predictions could reduce transfer overhead.

Library GP is more accurate but slower in this scalar-call CPU comparison. Timings exclude fitting/loading and final verification; NN training and data-generation times are unavailable. Repair also computes analytical RMA quantities internally. These results do not establish an end-to-end speedup over the inexpensive analytical formula.

7 Conclusion

GA, NN and PSO produce analytically valid designs near 2.4 GHz and 1057 mm^3. GP supplies an accurate library benchmark. CPU inference reduces NN latency. Global optimality and radiation performance are not established.
